# 14. Conclusion

A home is easier to care for when you can tell a manageable task from a change to a system you cannot fully see or test. The colour rule is a way to make that distinction before a purchase, a drilled hole, or a disconnected fitting. Green gives a beginner room to learn through bounded, visible work. Yellow calls for conditions written down in advance. Red asks for a good homeowner brief and qualified execution.

The rule starts with the **actual scope**. “Replace four cabinet handles using the existing holes” is specific enough to plan and inspect. “Improve the kitchen” is not. If the handles need new holes, the wall behind a shelf is uncertain, or a tap valve will not close, stop and classify the new job. Past success with a different flat, wall, or fitting cannot answer the current question. The same discipline applies when a professional proposes a change: agree on the revised scope, approval, test, price, and record before work proceeds.

Keep the three authority questions separate. Is the work allowed under current HDB rules? Does it need an HDB permit or have technical conditions? Must a licensed or approved person perform it? Then ask the practical risk questions: Can you control the hazard, recognise failure quickly, and restore the original condition? The colour is useful only after those checks. No amount of DIY enthusiasm changes a licensing rule, and a “no permit required” answer does not settle safety.

Start with one small project whose result you can see. Use the risk assessment in Appendix B, the permit and worker check in Appendix C, and the project plan in Appendix H. Photograph the starting condition. Write down the stop signs. After the work, check the result in normal use and keep the product details and receipt. For a larger task, the same one-page plan becomes a contractor brief: desired outcome, symptoms, site conditions, exclusions, required approval, test, and handover documents.

The strongest DIY habit is the willingness to change course when the evidence changes. A stain that returns after caulking calls for diagnosis. A hot switch calls for an electrical worker. A ceiling leak affecting another flat calls for coordination and a documented repair. Stopping early protects more than the repair budget; it protects other people and preserves the chance to find the real cause.

Use the skills you gain to make better decisions, maintain the parts of the flat you can safely reach, and hire well for the parts you cannot. That is the book's promise in one line: **Own the brief. Hire the risk.**
