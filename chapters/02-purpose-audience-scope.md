# 2. Purpose, Audience, and Scope

This book is for a Singapore homeowner living in an HDB flat who wants to care for the home without taking on avoidable risk. It assumes curiosity, ordinary hand tools, and little or no trade experience. You may want to save money, make a room work better, or understand a contractor's quotation. Those are good reasons to learn. They are not reasons to ignore a hidden hazard or a licensing rule.

The book covers small, bounded residential maintenance and improvement jobs. It explains how to judge a task before buying materials, how to recognise a stop point, and how to brief a professional when the job is beyond DIY. The colour labels describe the *specific scope*: cleaning a showerhead is different from altering water pipes; putting together a freestanding cabinet is different from fixing it to an uncertain wall; replacing a bulb is different from changing a lighting point.

The primary setting is an owner-occupied HDB flat. Rules for condominium units, landed homes, rented flats, common property, and commercial premises may differ. If your situation falls outside the primary setting, use the risk questions but check the rules that apply to your property before acting. In an HDB flat, an item that does not need an HDB renovation permit may still have technical conditions or require a licensed worker.[^hdb-permit]

The book does not teach gas service work, electrical installation work, structural work, refrigerant-system work, or the repair of concealed waterproofing. It does not teach asbestos, lead, or substantial mould remediation. It will help you describe such problems, preserve useful evidence, identify the right class of professional, and understand the questions to ask before work starts. A reader should never treat a project profile as a substitute for HDB approval, a regulator's current requirements, the product instructions, or a qualified site assessment.

The method is practical risk management. For each candidate project, judge how likely a mistake is, how severe its outcome could be, whether a problem would be noticed soon, whether it can be reversed, and what skill, tools, permission, and testing are needed. The same method appears in the project profiles, decision form, and case studies. It is deliberately conservative about hidden harm: a small saving cannot justify a failure that could injure someone or damage another flat.

You can use this book in two ways. If a task fits a green or carefully bounded yellow profile, use the profile to plan and check the job. If it falls outside that scope, use the same questions to write a better brief, compare quotes, and record the result. In both cases, the homeowner remains responsible for making an informed decision.

The next chapter turns those questions into a colour decision. Keep the scope of one real project in mind as you read it.
