# 8. Decision Tools and Checklists

A checklist is useful only if it changes a decision. Use the tools in this chapter before buying materials or booking a worker, then revisit them if the scope changes. A blank answer is a reason to pause, not a box to tick optimistically.

## 8.1 The Green/Yellow/Red Check

Start with the job you actually intend to do. Can you see and reach all of the work? Could you discover wiring, water pipes, structural elements, or waterproofing behind it? Can you keep other people away and protect the flat? Is the result visible and testable? Could a mistake affect a neighbour? If the answer changes after work begins, classify the new job again.

Next, check authority. Look for the exact task in current HDB guidance and identify any permit, condition, or prohibition. Separately check whether EMA, PUB, or another authority requires a licensed person. HDB's permit page distinguishes permit decisions from the need to engage appropriately licensed workers, and PUB distinguishes listed simple plumbing work from regulated plumbing.[^hdb-permit][^pub-plumbing] Record the page, date, and result rather than relying on a remembered rule.

Finally, check capability. Do you understand the hazard, have the correct tools, know a safe stop point, and have a way to verify the result? A green task meets all of these with low consequences. A yellow task requires specific training and controls written in advance. A red task has a serious or hidden failure path, requires professional execution, or cannot be safely bounded by the homeowner.

## 8.2 The Pre-Project Risk Assessment

Appendix B provides a one-page form. Fill it out in plain language. Name the task and explicitly name what you will *not* touch. List the most credible failure, not a vague phrase such as “be careful.” Record the HDB rule and the regulator or licensed-worker check, with links or notes. Identify shutoffs and the person you would call if the job stops. Photograph the starting condition. If the form does not fit on one page, the project may need to be divided into smaller scopes.

The form is not an approval. It is a record of why you believe this particular scope is suitable. A new leak, damaged cable, wet substrate, loose support, or unfamiliar fitting invalidates that assessment. Stop, make the area safe, and classify the expanded problem before continuing.

## 8.3 Permit and Licensed-Worker Check

Appendix C separates four decisions that are easy to confuse: HDB permission, technical conditions, licensed execution, and completion evidence. For example, HDB lists some electrical works that do not need its permit, but EMA still requires a Licensed Electrical Worker for electrical installation work.[^hdb-electrical][^ema-electrical] The form also asks whether the work involves common property or another flat, and which party will submit any required application.

Do this check early enough to change the project plan. A permit can affect timing; a licensed worker can affect scope and quote; product instructions can affect the materials you buy. If the work is absent from HDB's published lists or differs from a listed detail, HDB says to seek its approval before starting.[^hdb-permit]

## 8.4 Stop, Secure, and Call

Write a stop-work trigger for every yellow task. Examples include water that will not shut off, a fitting that moves unexpectedly, concealed damage, a burning smell, a repeated breaker trip, a structural crack, or water appearing outside the intended area. Do not cover up a problem with paint, sealant, or a larger fitting. Record what you found and contact the relevant qualified person.

Appendix D is a flat-specific shutoff and contact map. Complete it in daylight, before there is an emergency. HDB's home-care guidance identifies the stopcock at the PUB water sub-meter as the supply cut-off for a leakage; your map should record where yours is and whether it is accessible.[^hdb-pipes] For suspected gas leakage, use current gas-emergency guidance rather than troubleshooting the appliance yourself.[^ema-gas]
