# Four-week manuscript drafting sprint

## Sprint goal

Turn the 15-section outline and eight appendix stubs into a coherent, sourced first manuscript that can be assembled and proofed as a LaTeX PDF. The sprint ends with a reviewed draft and a PDF proof; publication follows only after jurisdiction-specific and trade-safety review.

**Working assumptions:** one lead writer/editor, 20 working days, roughly 18,000–22,000 words plus tables and forms. The book is for first-time DIY homeowners in Singapore HDB flats and uses metric units and Singapore dollars. A licensed or otherwise qualified reviewer checks regulated trades and life-safety content.

## Definition of done

- Every numbered section is prose, rather than an outline. Every named appendix is a usable table, form, checklist, glossary, reference list, or sample plan.
- The green/yellow/red rule is stated once, applied consistently in the project profiles, decision tools, cases, and master table. A permit requirement triggers a local rules check; it does not automatically make every project red.
- Each green or yellow project profile covers value, prerequisites, tools/materials, time/cost ranges with assumptions, hazards, stop-work signs, quality checks, and when to hire a professional. Red profiles explain risks and hiring questions without becoming execution tutorials.
- Factual safety, code, permit, insurance, warranty, and cost claims have traceable sources and dates. Local variations are named. Unsupported absolutes are revised or removed.
- A complete LaTeX source builds without errors; tables, links, references, headings, page breaks, and cross-references are checked in the PDF proof.

## Priority writing backlog

1. **Decision rule:** define how likelihood, severity, detectability, reversibility, code/insurance exposure, and required skill affect the color. Add examples where one red flag overrides a low score.
2. **Project profiles:** use one repeatable format across sections 5 and 6. Explain transitions, such as cosmetic tub caulk versus shower waterproofing, plug-in devices versus hardwired devices, and low shelves versus structural loads.
3. **Reader tools:** make the assessment form, master table, permit checklist, and stop-work list actionable and mutually consistent.
4. **Evidence:** source safety guidance from relevant authorities and manufacturers; source cost examples with location, date, and assumptions. Verify legal and insurance claims against the chosen jurisdiction and policies.
5. **Cases:** include an estimate, what happened, the decision boundary, and the lesson in each case. Clearly label illustrative or composite cases.

## Publication risks to resolve early

- The outline places some permitted work in yellow, then says “anything requiring a permit or inspection” is red. The book needs one rule that treats permit status as a serious decision factor and follows local law.
- “Insurance denied claims,” “permit often required,” and similar statements vary by policy and jurisdiction. Qualify them after review.
- Some green examples involve drilling, ladders, water, or appliance exhaust. Each profile needs a clear stop condition that moves the work to yellow or red.
- Prices and timelines are placeholders until tied to a market, date, scope, and source.

## LaTeX handoff conventions

- Keep one source file per numbered section and appendix through the drafting stage; make the same filenames the stable inputs to the manuscript build.
- Use a single heading hierarchy, unique labels for tables/figures, descriptive link text, and separate image assets with credit/license records.
- Store references in one bibliography with source URLs, access dates, jurisdiction, and claim notes. Avoid bare, unverified citations in prose.
- Freeze content before layout polish; after the freeze, change wording only to fix proof or review findings, then rebuild and inspect the PDF.
